# Econ Writing Base Rules

**Version:** 1.0  
**Status:** General fallback rules (Priority 3). Superseded by journal-specific SKILL.md where conflict exists.  
**Source:** Synthesized from Cochrane, McCloskey, Shapiro, Head, Bellemare, and related guides.

---

## Core Principle

Every sentence must earn its place. Cut everything that does not add information, evidence, or clarity.

---

## 1. Opening and Framing

- Lead with the economic question or phenomenon, not with "The literature on X has grown."
- Make the reader care before explaining your contribution.
- State what is at stake — the policy, welfare, or theoretical consequence — early.
- Do not open by surveying prior work. Open by establishing a problem.

## 2. Contribution Statements

- Be specific: name the mechanism, the quantitative result, or the methodological advance.
- Avoid: "contributes to the literature by providing a new framework for understanding X."
- Prefer: state the contribution as a finding — what you show, prove, or document.
- Do not list 5+ contributions. 2–3 sharp claims beat 5 vague ones.

## 3. Literature Positioning

- Position paper relative to the 3–5 most directly relevant papers, not the entire field.
- Explain WHY your paper is different, not just THAT it is different.
- Do not use "to the best of our knowledge" — if you've done your homework, state what you know.
- Literature review serves to motivate your gap, not to demonstrate reading.

## 4. Argumentation

- One idea per paragraph. Topic sentence states the paragraph's claim.
- Evidence follows claim. Do not bury the point at the end.
- Causal language requires causal identification. If no identification, use correlational language.
- Do not assert what you have not shown.

## 5. Results and Mechanisms

- Report the mechanism, not just the sign of the coefficient.
- Quantify when possible. "Large" and "small" are meaningless without context.
- Robustness checks belong after the main result, not before. Do not lead with robustness.
- Effect sizes matter as much as statistical significance.

## 6. Language

- Active voice preferred. Passive voice acceptable when the actor is irrelevant or unknown.
- Sentences under ~30 words preferred. Long sentences acceptable only when structure requires it.
- Cut all filler: "It is important to note that", "As mentioned above", "It should be noted."
- Cut hollow hedging: "somewhat", "rather", "fairly", "relatively" without benchmark.
- Keep strong hedging when genuine uncertainty exists: "we cannot rule out", "the mechanism is consistent with but does not prove."
- No rhetorical questions in academic prose.

## 7. Anti-AI-Taste Patterns (always remove)

These phrases signal AI-generated text and must be removed:

```
"This paper explores..."
"In this study, we aim to..."
"It is worth noting that..."
"Furthermore, / Moreover, / Additionally," (as filler transitions)
"This has important implications for..."
"...contributes to the growing literature on..."
"Taken together, these findings suggest..."
"Future research should explore..."
"In conclusion, this paper has shown..."
"Our results highlight the importance of..."
```

## 8. Section-Specific Defaults

**Abstract:** Context → Gap → Method → Main finding → Implication. Past tense for findings.

**Introduction:** Hook → problem + stakes → gap → contribution → (roadmap optional). ≤6 paragraphs.

**Methods:** Setup → assumptions (with justification) → key results. Intuition before formalism.

**Results:** Result → mechanism → robustness reference. Do not repeat the methods section.

**Discussion:** What do results mean for the broader question? What does this change?

**Conclusion:** Do not repeat the paper. State what you have learned and what it implies.
