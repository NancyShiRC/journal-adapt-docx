# Abstract — Revised v1

Battery swapping turns the battery in electric two-wheeler markets from a purchased component into a shared lifecycle asset. This paper studies who should own that asset under Battery-as-a-Service (BaaS), and how ownership should be coordinated when pricing, service investment, and battery recovery are jointly determined. We develop a Stackelberg game with an original equipment manufacturer (OEM), a swapping operator, and a coordinating platform, and compare OEM-owned, operator-owned, and platform-coordinated battery structures. The model endogenizes vehicle pricing, BaaS fees, swapping prices, service-level investment, battery buyback, and revenue- and cost-sharing terms. The analysis shows that the preferred owner is the actor with the lowest effective lifecycle cost, combining acquisition cost, degradation management, traceability, residual-value capture, and recovery responsibility. Decentralized ownership creates double marginalization and service-level underinvestment, whereas a contract combining revenue sharing, lifecycle-cost sharing, and battery buyback can coordinate the channel when lifecycle records are verifiable and participation constraints are satisfied. The simulations are disciplined by station POIs, official nighttime-light and population databases, and published charging, mobility, and battery studies; unobserved tariffs and contract terms are treated as transparent scenario parameters and examined through robustness checks.

Keywords: Electric two-wheelers, Battery swapping, Battery-as-a-Service, Battery ownership, Contract design, Stackelberg game, Supply chain management
