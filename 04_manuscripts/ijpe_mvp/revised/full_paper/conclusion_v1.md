# Conclusion — Revised v1

This paper studies battery ownership and contract design in an E2W BaaS swapping supply chain. The central result is that the preferred owner is defined by effective lifecycle cost rather than nominal asset possession or the lowest purchase price. The OEM may be the better owner when design information, warranty control, and recovery channels dominate. The operator may be the better owner when operational data, maintenance capability, and service responsiveness dominate. Platform coordination can improve on both decentralized structures when it aligns service incentives with lifecycle responsibility through revenue sharing, lifecycle-cost sharing, and battery buyback.

The model also explains why ownership and contracts cannot be separated. Battery ownership affects service level because higher service quality requires battery stock, maintenance effort, and lifecycle management. Decentralized ownership can create double marginalization, service underinvestment, and mispriced recovery incentives. A coordinating contract must therefore address three margins at once: downstream price, service investment, and recovery responsibility. The simulations, disciplined by station POIs, official spatial databases, and published charging, mobility, and battery studies, show how these mechanisms vary across city scenarios and robustness checks, while leaving private tariffs and contract terms as transparent scenario parameters.

The broader implication is that BaaS should be treated as a product-service-circularity system rather than a tariff innovation. For firms, the ownership decision should follow lifecycle capability: who can finance, monitor, maintain, retire, and recover batteries at the lowest effective cost while preserving service incentives? For policy makers, station deployment targets should be complemented by rules for battery identification, health-state records, data interoperability, recovery responsibility, and residual-value settlement. Future research can estimate rider demand and contract parameters from transaction records, embed queueing and stockout dynamics, and study multi-platform competition.
