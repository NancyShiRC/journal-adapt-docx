## Revised Section Text

This paper reframes battery ownership in E2W BaaS as a governance decision for a circulating asset. In a conventional vehicle sale, ownership mainly determines who pays for the component. In a swapping network, ownership also determines who finances battery stock, controls quality, invests in service level, captures residual value, and remains accountable for compliant recovery. Battery ownership is therefore part of the mechanism that produces service capacity, not a contract detail added after the network is built.

The first implication is that the best battery owner is not necessarily the party with the lowest purchase cost. It is the party with the lowest effective lifecycle cost. Effective lifecycle cost combines acquisition cost, capital cost, degradation management, maintenance, traceability cost, residual-value capture, and recovery responsibility. The OEM may have advantages in design, standardization, warranty, and recovery channels. The operator may have advantages in usage data, daily maintenance, station density, and demand response. The platform may reduce cross-party coordination costs through record sharing, settlement rules, and service standards. The ownership rule is therefore not who pays for the battery, but which party can manage the full battery lifecycle at lower effective cost.

The second implication concerns service investment. In the manufacturer-owned structure, the OEM retains battery ownership and may have stronger design and recovery capability, but the operator faces riders and chooses the service level. If the operator cannot capture enough of the system-level gain from better service, it underinvests in cabinet density, charged-battery availability, maintenance, or reliability. If the OEM also raises the operator's marginal burden through an authorization fee or battery-service fee, the channel suffers double marginalization. In the operator-owned structure, the operator is closer to users and can internalize the link between station density, swap reliability, and maintenance. However, operator ownership also exposes the operator to capital cost, degradation risk, and residual-value uncertainty. Operator ownership is attractive only when the operator's lifecycle-management advantage is large enough to offset those burdens.

The third implication is that platform coordination is valuable only when it coordinates price, service level, and recovery responsibility at the same time. A platform is not efficient merely because it intermediates traffic between OEMs and operators. Revenue sharing can reduce downstream price distortion. Lifecycle-cost sharing can improve service-investment incentives. Battery buyback or recovery transfer can strengthen the incentive to return retired batteries and allocate residual value. A single instrument usually solves only one part of the problem. E2W BaaS therefore requires a compound coordination mechanism that places product sales, service operations, and closed-loop recovery inside the same governance structure.

The simulation design reinforces this interpretation by distinguishing mature-network cities from high-demand but relatively underserved cities. In mature markets, the main problem is less about whether stations exist and more about improving reliability, reducing waiting and battery-shortage risk, and lowering degradation and maintenance cost under dense operation. In service-gap markets, the main issue is initial deployment and marginal service investment. Ownership matters more in such markets because a high service level requires more spare batteries, denser cabinets, and stronger maintenance capability. Platform coordination can be especially useful when revenue sharing and cost sharing reduce the expansion risk borne by a single operator, while lifecycle records and buyback terms reduce uncertainty after battery retirement.

For managers, the ownership decision should follow lifecycle capability rather than market power alone. OEMs should retain ownership when their advantages in design, standardization, warranty, health assessment, and compliant recovery outweigh the risk of weakening the operator's service incentives. Operators should own batteries when their operational data, maintenance capability, station knowledge, and real-time battery-health information give them a lifecycle-cost advantage. Platforms should coordinate ownership and service only when they can set standards, record lifecycle information, clear payments, and allocate recovery responsibility.

For policy makers, battery traceability and recovery regulation do not mechanically favor one ownership structure. They change the relative lifecycle-cost advantage of different actors. When regulation raises the importance of traceability, battery-health records, and compliant recovery, the actor with stronger data-recording capability, recovery channels, and residual-value realization gains an ownership advantage. Policy design should therefore look beyond station counts and subsidies. Battery identity coding, health-state records, data interoperability, recovery responsibility, and residual-value allocation rules directly affect service investment and circular-economy performance in BaaS systems.

The analysis has several limitations. First, the model uses a reduced-form service level to represent station coverage, available battery inventory, waiting time, and reliability. It does not explicitly embed a queueing model, stockout probability, or dynamic battery-dispatch process. Second, the simulations are data-disciplined numerical analysis rather than structural estimation from transaction data. POIs, nighttime lights, and population exposure describe spatial heterogeneity, but they do not identify user price elasticity, actual swap frequency, contract shares, battery transfer prices, or retired-battery residual values. Third, the model is static, whereas real BaaS systems evolve as batteries age, residual values change, markets expand, and regulation tightens. Finally, the model considers a stylized supply chain with one OEM, one operator, and one platform coordinator. These limitations do not alter the central message: in E2W BaaS swapping networks, battery ownership is a governance tool for service capacity and lifecycle responsibility, and the system performs best when price incentives, service investment, and recovery responsibility are coordinated jointly.
