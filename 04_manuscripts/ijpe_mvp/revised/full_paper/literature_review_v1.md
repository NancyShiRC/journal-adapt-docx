## Revised Section Text

### 2.1. Battery swapping operations and micromobility

Battery swapping is motivated by a mismatch between the time required to charge a battery and the time users are willing to spend on replenishment. A station or cabinet network separates these processes: the rider receives a charged battery immediately, while the operator charges, inspects, stores, and reallocates depleted batteries. This separation explains why much of the battery-swapping literature focuses on location, inventory, scheduling, dispatching, routing, and matching decisions. Recent reviews describe battery swapping as a system-level alternative to plug-in charging that can reduce downtime and support centralized battery management, while also noting that business-model and policy questions remain underexplored [1]. Related transportation and energy studies examine taxi adoption, strategic matching, service network design, station energy scheduling, rural energy-station planning, and robust location-inventory planning [3, 4, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12].

Micromobility and two-wheeler settings give battery swapping a different economic structure from electric buses, taxis, and heavy-duty trucks. Shared micromobility operators manage many small batteries and vehicles over dense urban space, making rebalancing, pickup and delivery, battery replacement, and service equity central decisions [5, 6, 7]. In motorcycle-taxi or electric-motorcycle contexts, such as boda-boda electrification, high daily utilization and costly downtime can make swapping preferable to long plug-in charging [2]. These studies show that E2W swapping is not simply a scaled-down version of car swapping. Battery packs are modular, service points can be dense, and the battery accounts for a substantial share of vehicle cost.

The operational literature nevertheless often treats ownership and contracting as fixed background conditions. A station-location model may assume that the operator already owns the cabinets and batteries. A routing model may take swapping as a predetermined service process. A user-adoption study may estimate preferences for swapping without specifying who bears battery capital cost or captures residual value. In E2W BaaS, these upstream choices are part of the operating economics. If the operator chooses cabinet density but does not own the battery, its service investment depends on the contract. If the OEM controls battery design while the operator observes battery health, lifecycle efficiency depends on data sharing and recovery terms. The E2W setting therefore requires a model that jointly treats ownership, pricing, service level, and lifecycle responsibility.

### 2.2. User adoption, service quality, and BaaS demand

Demand for battery swapping depends on price, time, battery quality, and trust. Discrete-choice evidence in EV contexts shows that users compare charging and swapping by cost, convenience, range, charging or swapping time, and battery-health concerns [3]. Driver-continuance studies also show that battery quality and service expectations affect repeat usage [4]. In E2W and micromobility markets, time costs can be especially important because commercial riders earn income from active vehicle use. A tariff that lowers the upfront vehicle price but raises monthly service charges may suit high-utilization riders, whereas low-utilization private riders may be more sensitive to subscription commitments.

BaaS turns the demand problem from a one-time product purchase into a product-service bundle. The rider evaluates a bare-vehicle price, a subscription or rental fee, a possible per-swap fee, deposit requirements, network coverage, and expected waiting time. The model therefore distinguishes the vehicle-side price from the energy-service price. This distinction is standard in service-operation models where customers respond to both monetary and time-related attributes. Classic service-competition research shows that firms competing on price, waiting time, capacity, and service quality may choose different positions depending on market segmentation and congestion [14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19]. Price competition under heterogeneous demand also depends on user segments, motivating segmented or logit demand extensions for commercial and private riders [20].

For the present paper, the key implication is that demand cannot be separated from ownership. The party that owns the battery also affects service level, battery quality assurance, and the cost passed through to users. The baseline model therefore uses a reduced-form service-level variable rather than a full queueing system. This variable captures the demand benefit of dense cabinets, sufficient charged-battery inventory, short replacement time, and reliable battery quality. The service-competition literature provides the economic interpretation: higher service level raises demand but requires costly capacity and operating effort [15, 18, 21, 22].

### 2.3. Battery-as-a-Service and ownership separation

BaaS separates vehicle ownership from battery access. A user may purchase a bare vehicle and then pay a monthly subscription, a per-swap price, or a hybrid tariff. This arrangement can reduce upfront cost and transfer degradation risk to professional battery managers. The separation matters in E2W markets because the battery represents a large fraction of vehicle cost, usage intensity varies widely, and riders may be concerned about safety and quality. It also creates a supply-chain design question: who should own the battery?

Ownership affects incentives on both sides of the market. If the OEM owns the batteries, it can internalize design, standardization, warranty, and recovery, and may have better information about chemistry, manufacturing quality, and pack design. If the swapping operator owns the batteries, it can internalize utilization, inventory, cabinet density, and maintenance because it observes charging and swapping histories directly. If a platform coordinates the system, it can improve demand access, settlement, lifecycle records, and cross-firm standardization, but it must allocate revenue and lifecycle costs across parties. BaaS ownership is therefore a joint decision about finance, information, asset control, residual value, and responsibility.

Although BaaS appears in studies of EV swapping and energy-service models, contract design remains underdeveloped for E2Ws. Existing swapping studies increasingly consider strategic matching and business models [8], and a recent heavy-duty-truck study uses a cooperative Stackelberg game for swapping pricing [13]. E2W BaaS differs because the battery is highly modular, the user base is segmented, and a dense cabinet network can be operated as a product-service ecosystem. The ownership question is therefore central to how price, service investment, and lifecycle responsibility are governed.

### 2.4. Supply-chain contracts and Stackelberg games

Stackelberg games are well suited to BaaS supply chains because one party often sets contract terms before another party chooses market-facing decisions. In an OEM-led structure, the OEM can set the bare-vehicle price, battery authorization fee, or wholesale battery price before the swapping operator chooses service prices and service level. In an operator-led structure, a dominant service provider may choose the service tariff and battery stock while negotiating procurement with vehicle or battery manufacturers. In a platform-coordinated structure, a platform may specify revenue sharing, service standards, settlement rules, and battery return rewards before downstream operations occur.

Supply-chain contract theory provides the central mechanism. Decentralized channels often suffer from double marginalization: an upstream mark-up raises the downstream price above the integrated optimum and reduces demand. Service underinvestment can also arise when the party investing in service level captures only part of the marginal demand benefit. Revenue-sharing contracts can reduce double marginalization by lowering the downstream marginal burden while allowing upstream firms to capture part of demand revenue [23, 24]. Buyback and return contracts can coordinate inventory or recovery incentives by allocating residual value and downside risk [25]. Closed-loop supply-chain studies further show that product returns, remanufacturing, and recycling depend on channel structure and recovery incentives [26, 27, 28, 29].

E2W BaaS combines these contract problems. The battery owner finances battery stock, controls degradation, and recovers residual value. The operator invests in cabinets, charging, and maintenance. The OEM sets vehicle terms and influences battery standardization. A pure wholesale contract can distort service level because each additional unit of cabinet availability may require additional battery stock. A pure revenue-sharing contract can coordinate price but leave recovery incentives weak. A pure buyback contract can improve recovery but may not solve service underinvestment. We therefore study a combined contract with revenue sharing, lifecycle-cost sharing, and battery buyback.

### 2.5. Battery lifecycle, residual value, and circular supply chains

Battery ownership is inseparable from lifecycle responsibility. The owner decides how the pack is monitored, maintained, retired from swapping use, and transferred to recycling or appropriate second-life applications. Closed-loop supply-chain research shows that return channels and recovery incentives can affect both profitability and environmental performance [26, 27, 29]. In battery systems, residual value depends on state of health, chemistry, safety, traceability, and the market value of recovered materials or second-life applications. Machine-learning and battery-health studies highlight the value of estimating state of health and classifying batteries for reuse or recycling, supporting the idea that lifecycle information has economic value [30, 31, 32].

For E2W swapping, lifecycle management is more controllable than in individually owned batteries because packs pass through swapping cabinets frequently. Centralized charging and inspection can generate detailed usage records, reducing uncertainty about degradation and residual value. The economic value of this information, however, depends on ownership and contracts. If the operator observes battery health but the OEM owns the battery, the parties need data-sharing and settlement rules. If the operator owns the battery but the OEM controls design and warranty, the operator may underinvest in recovery unless buyback terms are credible. If a platform coordinates records and settlement, it can reduce verification frictions only when parties agree on how recovered value is allocated.

Lifecycle responsibility enters the model through effective lifecycle cost. This cost includes acquisition, stock intensity, degradation, maintenance, recovery processing, compliance cost, and residual value. The actor with the lowest effective lifecycle cost is not necessarily the actor with the lowest purchase cost. It may instead be the actor with better monitoring, residual-value capture, or recovery capability. This lifecycle-cost perspective puts OEM ownership, operator ownership, and platform coordination on a common basis.

### 2.6. Research gap and positioning

The literature leaves four gaps. First, battery-swapping operations research offers rich station, inventory, routing, scheduling, and matching models, but gives less attention to ownership and contract design for E2W BaaS. Second, BaaS adoption studies identify user concerns about price, time, and battery quality, but rarely connect these demand factors to supply-chain ownership. Third, service-competition models provide tools for price and service-level decisions, yet they do not incorporate battery lifecycle cost and residual value. Fourth, closed-loop supply-chain models study return and recovery contracts, but they do not fully capture a modular battery-service setting in which the same battery asset repeatedly circulates through a swapping network.

This paper connects these streams by treating E2W BaaS as a product-service supply chain built around a recoverable asset. The model compares three ownership structures and derives how ownership affects price, service level, demand, and profit. It also explains why a combined contract is needed: revenue sharing addresses price distortion, cost sharing addresses service-level investment, and buyback addresses battery recovery.
